Gungnir: Autoregressive Model for Unified
Generation of IPv6 Fully Responsive Prefixes

Chentian Wei∗†, Ying Liu∗†, Lin He∗†, Daguo Cheng∗†, Jiasheng Zhou∗†
∗Tsinghua University, †Zhongguancun Laboratory

Abstract—With the widespread adoption of IPv6,

its vast
address space presents significant challenges for network asset
discovery. Traditional exhaustive scanning approaches are no
longer practical, while strategies based on target generation
algorithms are increasingly undermined by the existence of
Fully Responsive Prefixes (FRPs)—prefixes in which all addresses
appear responsive to probing. FRPs distort scanning results, in-
troducing bias and inefficiency. Existing FRP probing techniques
suffer from limited scalability, poor accuracy, and restricted
applicability in large-scale IPv6 environments.

To this end, we propose Gungnir, a multi-protocol unified FRP
probing algorithm based on autoregressive semantic modeling.
Gungnir captures the intricate relationships between FRP pat-
terns and their influencing factors through a deep semantic learn-
ing architecture. It leverages prefix inference and a granularity
correction mechanism to accurately predict and validate FRPs,
while mitigating errors from incorrect prefix-length estimation.
Extensive experiments demonstrate that Gungnir outperforms
state-of-the-art techniques, achieving up to 27× higher efficiency,
4.2× wider address space coverage, and broader coverage of both
autonomous systems and routing prefixes under the same probing
budget. Beyond performance, we further analyze the service and
port distributions of the discovered FRPs, uncovering operational
patterns and potential security implications. These insights offer
valuable guidance for IPv6 measurement, address discovery, and
network defense.

I. INTRODUCTION

Internet-wide network scanning, as the core technology of
cyberspace resource mapping, obtains the response character-
istics of the target network by actively sending customized
probe packets. For example, tools such as ZMap [1] enable
high-performance active address scanning. It provides basic
data support for researching Internet infrastructure security [2],
[3], conducting geolocation analysis [4], and analyzing the
impact of the Internet on the real world [5], and plays a crucial
role in the field of network measurement [6]–[10]. However,
with the large-scale deployment of the IPv6 protocol [11],
the huge space brought by its 128-bit address makes network
scanning face new challenges. Within this space lies a special
class of prefixes—Fully Responsive Prefixes (FRPs)—which
span large ranges where every address appears responsive to
probing [12], [13]. For example, if all addresses within a target
prefix respond to ICMPv6 Echo messages, the prefix can be
classified as an FRP for ICMPv6. Nevertheless, it is evident
that such responses cannot originate from a single host.

FRPs have significantly influenced the widely used target
generation algorithms (TGAs) [14]–[22] employed in active
address probing—one of the current research hotspots. For

The widespread deployment of FRPs also reflects various
intentional uses: running multiple services on a single server,
enabling CDN providers to implement server-side load bal-

scanning methodologies. Accurate identification and handling
of FRPs not only improve the fidelity of scanning outcomes
but also lay the groundwork for their constructive application
in network research and operations.

Previous research efforts [26] have identified the presence
of such prefixes in active address probing. To mitigate their
impact, a method known as MAPD [12] was proposed for
identifying these prefixes. However, MAPD fundamentally
relies on an exhaustive generation approach that produces a
set of random candidate addresses for probing—a strategy
that becomes impractical given the vastness of the IPv6
address space. Furthermore, MAPD is limited to detecting
seed addresses (i.e., known active IPv6 addresses), rendering
it ineffective for comprehensive, Internet-wide FRP discovery.
To overcome these limitations, Luori [25] efficiently uncovers
FRPs via active probing. It formulates FRP detection as a
reinforcement learning problem, leveraging Monte Carlo Tree
Search (MCTS) to guide probing and improve efficiency.

Despite recent advancements, FRP probing continues to
face several key challenges: (1) Enormous Decision Space.
FRP probing requires the identification of target prefixes
within the vast IPv6 address space. Existing methods such
as MAPD [12], which relies on exhaustive enumeration of
prefixes with fixed lengths, and Luori [25], which adopts
a tree-based approach for layer-by-layer expansion, struggle
with accurately predicting both the lengths and patterns of
FRPs. These limitations lead to inefficiencies, especially when
incorrect prefix lengths are assumed. For instance, mistakenly
treating a /64 prefix as a /80 can result in significant resource
waste and introduce substantial noise into the dataset due
to unnecessary probing. (2) Multi-Protocol and Port-Type
Probing Complexity. Previous work [12], [25] also falls short

II. BACKGROUND AND RELATED WORK

A. Fully Responsive Prefixes

Before the formalization of FRPs, Murdock et al. [26] ob-
served a notable phenomenon in IPv6 active address probing:
a significant proportion of addresses within the same prefix
responded uniformly to Internet-wide scans, a characteristic
they termed aliased prefixes. Their active probing experiments
revealed that 98% of responsive addresses resided in regions
exhibiting large-scale aliasing, despite these addresses ulti-
mately resolving to the same host or network service via FRPs
configured across different protocol ports [27]. Building on
this discovery, Zirngibl et al. [13] rigorously defined FRPs as
prefixes demonstrating universal address responsiveness (i.e.,
a 100% probe reply rate), while critically differentiating them
from aliased prefixes by relaxing the constraint of single-host
control. This conceptual framework establishes aliased pre-
fixes as a proper subset of FRPs—a hierarchical relationship
consistently maintained in our subsequent analysis. A parallel
phenomenon exists in IPv4 networks, where Sattler et al. [27]
identified highly responsive prefixes (HRPs). These prefixes
exhibit responsiveness from over 90% of their constituent
addresses for specific ports. Unlike their IPv6 counterparts,
IPv4 HRPs may encompass multiple hosts, including content
delivery networks (CDNs) or TCP proxy infrastructures.

Recent research has increasingly focused on the measure-
ment biases introduced by these phenomena. Oliver Gasser et
al. [12] demonstrated that aliased prefixes artificially inflate
hitlist address counts by mapping multiple entries to single
devices, thereby distorting assessments of network scale and
topology. Similarly, Lion Steger et al. [28] identified aliased
prefixes as a critical consideration in IPv6 measurement
methodologies.

B. FRP Probing

For FRP probing, earlier studies [26] employed a straight-
forward method to detect aliased prefixes. First, they selected
the /96 prefix containing the responding probe addresses, then
randomly generated three addresses within that prefix. Three
TCP SYN probes were sent to each address, and if all three
responded to at least one probe, the prefix was deemed aliased.
However, this approach introduces bias, prompting Gasser et
al. to propose Aliased Prefix Detection (APD) [12]. APD
enhances reliability by randomly selecting 16 addresses within
the 0-f sub-prefix of a candidate prefix. If all 16 addresses
respond, the prefix is labeled as aliased. Subsequent FRP
probing efforts have largely adopted the APD methodology,
evolving into MAPD [14], [17], [21], [25], [29]. MAPD
exhaustively enumerates all possible candidate prefixes and
applies aliased prefix probing. As noted by [25], these methods
do not verify whether the probed addresses resolve to the same
host, effectively making them FRP probing techniques rather
than strict alias detection methods.

Most recently, Luori [25] introduced an active probing
framework for FRP that leverages MCTS to transform the
vast IPv6 search space into a reinforcement learning decision-
making process. While effective, its hierarchical tree expansion

III. PROBLEM ANALYSIS AND DEFINITION

The purpose of FRP probing is to identify as many FRPs
as possible within a given target routing prefix, building upon
the active address probing framework proposed in [30] by first
analyzing the structural patterns of FRPs. FRPs consist of
two main components—the fixed routing prefix (determined
by BGP announcements) and variable FRP patterns—and our
analysis focuses on the latter. We model these patterns as a
prefix tree where the root represents the routing prefix and
each subsequent layer corresponds to binary expansions of
the pattern, revealing distinct distributional characteristics that
we quantify through the novel concept of pattern entropy.
This approach enables systematic exploration of FRP patterns
while accounting for their hierarchical structure and variability
across the prefix tree.

begin with an overview of the approach, followed by a detailed
explanation of each module’s design.

to capture the patterns of known types of FRP, enabling
efficient discovery of FRPs in the wild across different
protocols and ports. The FRP probing process of Gungnir
consists of three steps: semantic learning, prefix inference,

Gungnir performs online scanning of these candidate FRPs,

Embedding. The embedding layer provides a high-

model to capture rich semantic information. To effectively
learn the complex relationships among protocol and port types,
routing prefixes, belonging ASes, affiliated organizations, and
business types, Gungnir uses a long sequence for semantic
learning. The tokenizer begins by converting the target prefixes
into a sequence of tokens, while integrating relevant contextual
information, including BGP route announcements, business
relationships, and other network attributes. In the tokeniza-
tion process, the system first assigns sequential identifiers to
each token within the sequence information (for example,
Cloudflare ID: 124). It then maintains a mapping table that
associates these numerical identifiers with their corresponding
high-dimensional vector representations, establishing a con-
sistent reference framework for downstream processing. In
the process of mapping FRPs to tokens, FRPs are expanded
into a binary form representation to ensure that the model
can perform more fine-grained learning and prediction. The
composition of FRPs is divided into a routing prefix part
and an FRP pattern part. These high-dimensional embedding
vectors are learnable parameters during semantic learning.
To preserve and leverage the sequential nature of the data,
positional encoding P E is added to the token embeddings
T E, resulting in the final input embeddings IE that are fed
into the model. This design enables the model to effectively

1) Why Autoregressive Model: The FRP pattern is inher-
ently characterized by sequential dependency, where each bit
in the prefix depends on preceding bits—a property stem-
ming from IPv6 addressing architecture [34]. Autoregressive
models [35] effectively capture this sequential dependence.
Moreover, it provides a unified framework for FRP predic-
tion across multiple protocols and port types. As detailed in
§III, autoregressive models optimize the alignment between
predicted and actual FRP distributions by minimizing KL
divergence.

2) Why Transformer Model: The self-attention mechanism
in Transformers excels at modeling complex relationships
between FRP patterns and influencing factors (e.g., AS num-
bers, organization types). The multi-head attention mecha-
nism (§IV-B) enables simultaneous focus on semantic features
at different positions, which is crucial for learning cross-
organizational FRP distribution patterns. Additionally, com-
pared to RNN architectures [36], Transformers offer superior
parallel computing capabilities, significantly improving pro-
cessing efficiency for large-scale IPv6 address spaces.

C. Prefix Inference

In semantic learning, the model has learned FRP patterns
under multiple relational attributes, and next, the model needs
to generate FRPs for the given conditions. Previous work, such
as Luori [25], only predicts single-type information and only
for a single active type FRP. To allow the model to generate
patterns of FRPs based on joint probabilities given multiple
conditions, we employ prefix inference. As shown in Figure
5, its decision process can be viewed as a Markov chain, and

Granularity Correction. As Erdemir et al. [23] found, the
current FRP dataset, e.g., the well-known IPv6 hitlist [12],
suffers from a large number of prefix granularity errors. For
example, a large number of /48 and /56 prefixes are incorrectly
categorized as /64 FRPs. The deviation of the prefix length
distribution in the dataset from the real web environment
where FRPs are deployed affects the performance of the
prediction methods, e.g., Gungnir for semantic learning learns
the wrong sequence distributions, which generates this noise
in the prefix inference. To solve this problem, we design the
granularity correction mechanism. By correcting the length of
existing prefixes, FRPs closer to the true length distribution are
obtained. As shown in Figure 6, given the initial correction
length lc, the length of the probed FRPs is gradually taken
shorter and lc is decayed by half in each round.

V. EVALUATION

A. Experimental Setting

1) Dataset: We utilized 227,894 IPv6 BGP routing prefixes
from the RouteViews project [37] as target prefixes, along with
Luori’s publicly available FRP dataset [25] as the initial seed
set. This dataset contains FRP on multiple ports, including
ICMPv6, TCP/22, and TCP/443. Notably, the ICMPv6 class
exhibits the highest activity level, comprising 182,942 active
FRPs distributed across 4,643 routing prefixes, representing
both the largest count and spatial coverage among all cate-
gories.

2) Hyper-parameters: The model employs a 6-layer Trans-
former decoder architecture with 128-dimensional hidden
states and 8 attention heads, trained using the Adam optimizer
with an initial learning rate of 0.001, batch size of 512, for 50
epochs. During prefix inference, we maintain the same batch
size (512) with a sampling probability p = 0.6. To ensure fair
comparison, all methods probe 16 randomly selected addresses
within candidate FRP prefixes, and a prefix is confirmed as an
FRP only when all 16 addresses respond positively.

3) Hardware: The experiments were conducted on a host
machine equipped with an Intel(R) Xeon(R) Gold 6230R CPU
(2.10 GHz), 256 GB of system memory, and a 100 Mbps
network connection. For both model training and inference,
we utilized a single NVIDIA RTX 4090 GPU with 24 GB of
memory.

B. Evaluation Metrics

• CovSpace: This metric quantifies the address space occu-
pied by FRPs while normalizing for prefix length varia-
tions. For instance, a /48 prefix and sixteen /52 prefixes
each cover 280 addresses (2128−48 = 16 × 2128−52),
demonstrating equivalent spatial coverage despite differ-
ing prefix lengths. By eliminating length-dependent bi-
ases, CovSpace provides an unbiased measure of address
space utilization. In FRP probing evaluations, discovering
more FRPs inherently requires identifying more and
shorter prefixes—making CovSpace a critical perfor-
mance metric that directly reflects algorithmic efficiency
in capturing fine-grained address blocks.

C. Performance Evaluation

1) Probing Effect: Table I and Table II show the probing re-
sults under ICMPv6, TCP/80 type for three different methods:
MAPD, Luori, and Gungnir. Bpref ix is the predicted budget
under each routing prefix(i.e., the number of probes under a
single prefix b ≤ Bpref ix), while BT otal is the budget used in
the actual probing process. We adopt a probing methodology
similar to prior work [14], [17], [21], [25], [29], where one
budget allows sending probes to 16 addresses within a single
prefix. The choice of the three budgets Bpref ix = [10, 30, 50]
is motivated by two factors: (1) prior studies [25] have adopted
similar budget values for fair comparison, and (2) these
settings enable FRP probing across target prefixes within a
manageable and controlled timeframe.

As shown in Table I, Gungnir consistently outperforms both
MAPD and Luori across all tested prefix budgets (Bpref ix =
[10, 30, 50]) in terms of routing prefix coverage (CovRouting)
and autonomous system coverage (CovAS). At Bpref ix = 10,
Gungnir achieves CovRouting = 1158, increasing the cov-
erage by 2.8 times and 1.7 times compared to Luori and
MAPD, respectively. This performance gap further widens
at Bpref ix = 50, where Gungnir’s CovRouting = 1, 903
more than doubles MAPD’s result. The coverage space met-
ric (CovSpace) demonstrates even more striking advantages:
Gungnir maintains a consistent
lead of 3.7 − 4.2× over
Luori and 4.5 × 107 − 1.4 × 108 over MAPD. Notably, at

Bpref ix = 50, Gungnir achieves CovSpace = 1.92×1031, sur-
passing all other methods by significant margins. These results
strongly validate the effectiveness of our granularity correction
algorithm in precisely determining FRP granularities.

Table II shows the budget and coverage when using different
methods for network probing under TCP/80. Again, Gungnir
shows significant advantages at all budgets. At Bpref ix = 10,
CovRouting of Gungnir is 574, which is 3.4 times that of Luori
and 1.9 times that of MAPD. When Bpref ix increases by 50,
CovRouting of Gungnir reaches 981, which is 1.5 times that
of Luori. Meanwhile, the CovSpace of Gungnir stays maximal
under all budgets, which is 2.4-3.0 times higher than that of
Luori, while that of MAPD ranges from 1.54 × 1022 − 2.12 ×
1030 times.

Due to differing budget structures among the three meth-
ods, we employ a Pareto chart (Figure 7) for performance
comparison, with the y-axis representing actual budget usage
(BT otal). Figures 7a and 7b present results for ICMPv6
and TCP/80 across CovRouting/CovAS and CovSpace met-
rics respectively. Superior methods are indicated by curves
closer to the upper-left corner, denoting greater coverage per
budget. Gungnir demonstrates consistent dominance across all
metrics, maintaining strong performance even at low budgets
(Bpref ix = 10) through its flexible length prediction strategy.
In contrast, Luori’s layer-by-layer search tree approach under-
performs MAPD at minimal budgets due to its rigid expansion
mechanism.

2) Operating Time: Table III compares the performance
of both Luori and Gungnir in terms of operating time.
The experiment assigns 50 budgets to each routing prefix
across the Internet, and the average of multiple experiments
is taken to count the running time. The pre-training time
and inference time counted here refer to the total time, and
N refers to the number of target protocols and ports (e.g.,
TCP/80). Since Luori can only build a tree for a single active
type, Luori’s method takes about 4.9 × N hours for pre-
training, while Gungnir uses global information for semantic
learning, so Gungnir takes only 6.8 hours in total, and the
global information used also contributes to the accuracy and
comprehensiveness of the predictions. In the inference phase,
Luori’s method takes about 85 × N hours, while the Gungnir
method takes only about 2.5 × N hours, achieving ×34
increase in inference times on just a single active type. This
means that if the FRP is probed for 5 active types, Gungnir
can achieve nearly 7 times improvement in pre-training time
compared to Luori, and more than 27 times improvement in
total execution time. This shows the higher efficiency and more
comprehensive probing capability of Gungnir.

D. Value of the Hyperparameter p

The sampling probability p serves as a crucial parameter
in Gungnir’s prediction mechanism, controlling the trade-off
between pattern exploration and exploitation. Lower values
of p (approaching 0) cause Gungnir to predominantly repro-
duce patterns similar to those in the original dataset, while
higher values (approaching 1) drive the system to predict
novel patterns. To systematically evaluate this parameter’s
impact, we conducted experiments using uniformly sampled
values of p across the [0,1] interval, specifically testing at
p ∈ {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}. Since the ICMPv6 tends to be the most

active in the probing of FRP, we selected it as our baseline
protocol and performed all predictions within BGP-announced
routing prefixes. Each routing prefix was predicted 10 times in
our experiments, with FRPs subsequently filtered out from the
results. The experimental results for different hyperparameter
values of p are presented in Table IV. When p is set to 0.2
or 0.4, the model demonstrates inferior performance on both
CovAS and CovRouting metrics compared to values of 0.6 and
0.8, as these lower values constrain the model to generate FRPs
primarily within already learned patterns. Notably, p values of
0.6 and 0.8 yield comparable results for both coverage metrics.
To conduct a more comprehensive comparison, we em-
ployed the relative metric CovpA≥pB , which evaluates the
spatial sizes of results across different routing prefixes while
mitigating the influence of excessively short FRPs on spatial
measurements. Figure 8 illustrates this comparison, where the
axis represents set B and the four lines correspond to set A
with pA values of {0.2, 0.4, 0.6, 0.8}. The analysis reveals
that Covp0.6≥pB for pA = 0.6 generally exceeds Covp0.8≥pB
for pA = 0.8, except when pB = 0.8. This exception
occurs because the metric inherently favors cases where sets
A and B are identical. Importantly, in all other comparisons,
pA = 0.6 demonstrates superior performance to pA = 0.8,
both before and after granularity correction. These findings
suggest that while p = 0.8 produces larger FRP spaces, these
gains are likely attributable to a limited number of shorter
FRPs. This interpretation is supported by Table IV, which
shows that p = 0.8 fails to significantly improve CovAS and
CovRouting metrics and underperforms p = 0.6 in some cases.
Consequently, we recommend using p = 0.6 as the optimal
sampling value for prefix inference tasks.

F. Long-term Multi-protocol Probing

Through continuous Internet-wide monitoring spanning over
three months, we have compiled the most comprehensive FRP
dataset to date. Our collection encompasses 23 distinct active
protocol types, including ICMPv6, TCP, and UDP, represent-
ing the broadest coverage and largest address space currently
available. As demonstrated in Table V, comparative analysis
reveals that our dataset significantly outperforms existing FRP
lists across three key metrics: (1) coverage space, (2) number
of covered routing prefixes, and (3) count of included ASes,
with ICMPv6 serving as the primary benchmark for these
comparisons.

G. FRP Deployment

We conduct a comprehensive analysis of the Internet-wide
FRP distribution across multiple protocols, employing both
quantitative and spatial metrics to evaluate deployment scales.
As detailed in Appendix Tables VII and IX, the distribution
patterns reveal significant variations across major protocols.
Notably, three major organizations—Cloudflare, Amazon, and
Akamai—dominate the ICMPv6 FRP count, collectively ac-
counting for over 2 million FRPs. Their deployment strategies
contrast sharply with organizations like SIXNET: while the lat-
ter occupies vast address spaces, Cloudflare and Amazon im-
plement fine-grained subnet segmentation, configuring dense
FRP distributions within smaller prefixes. This aligns with
known implementations like Linux’s net.ipv6.ip nonlocal bind
option [25], which enables single-host gateway deployments
with prefix-wide address binding. Currently, we have identified
these deployed FRPs within at least 9 ASes by email and
have not received any error feedback from any of the ASes,
indicating that our measurements are true and reliable.

I. FRP Service Analysis

To understand why organizations widely deploy FRPs with
open ports, we analyzed services under 102,314 prefixes.
Using Zgrab2 [38], we probed 1.6 million generated addresses
to identify active services. Appendix Table VIII shows service
distribution patterns across major providers, revealing their
operational needs behind FRP configurations.

1) HTTP: We observe that certain ISPs (e.g., Internet
Rimon and Hughes) and hosting providers (e.g., WOOD-
SNET) expose port 80 but do not provide HTTP services.
We know from surveys of email and other methods that many
organizations will use the addresses under FRP as an address
pool, and at the egress gateway, the address pool will assign
a proxy ip for the egress service as a random proxy ip for

the outbound service, and in this way can greatly alleviate the
problem of a single IP limiting [39], [40] the speed of the
service. This may explain why there is such a huge address
space open for specific ports, but no corresponding service
configuration. However, this FRP rate-limiting bypass strategy
also means that
the traditional single-ip rate-limiting and
DDoS defense strategy is invalidated, and the traditional IPv4-
based security protection mechanisms (such as ACL rules)
may not be able to effectively identify the abnormal traffic
of IPv6 fully responsive prefixes, which may generate security
risks. It is important to note that if these hosts only open ports
without actually providing any services, this port exposure to
the public network is likely to be a security risk.

Among the generated addresses, 233, 198(15%) were suc-
cessfully identified as hosting HTTP services. These respon-
sive addresses spanned 2,345 routing prefixes managed by 183
distinct organizations with confirmed HTTP service deploy-
ments. Notably, Akamai, Amazon, and Incapsula emerged as
the top three providers—a finding consistent with our prior
analysis. This alignment reinforces our observation that CDNs
frequently implement FRPs by enabling HTTP (port 80) across
all addresses within a prefix. Such configurations serve as a
critical mechanism for these networks to efficiently deliver
outbound services at scale.

2) SSH: Among the SSH services scanned, 48,120 gen-
erated addresses were successfully identified, spanning 573
routing prefixes across 184 autonomous systems (ASes). Our
analysis revealed several security risks, including instances
running outdated versions (e.g., OpenSSH 7.2p2) that retain
support for deprecated cryptographic algorithms such as diffie-
hellman-group14-sha1 [41]. A significant portion of these
FRPs likely belongs to a honeypot system designed to dy-
namically map unused IPv6 addresses to trap servers for attack
monitoring and analysis. In this setup, when an attacker scans a
network segment, unregistered address requests are redirected
to a honeypot, which consolidates multiple trap addresses into
a single service IP. This architecture enables efficient aggrega-
tion of attack traffic across a vast address space—explaining
the observed concentration of similarly configured hosts under
shared prefixes. The deliberate retention of OpenSSH 7.2p2
may serve as an active lure, exploiting attackers’ propensity
to target known vulnerabilities (e.g., CVE-2017-15906). By
maintaining compatibility with weak algorithms (e.g., diffie-
hellman-group14-sha1), the honeypot could further act as a
protocol-level decoy, capitalizing on scanning tools’ tendency
to prioritize such algorithms to enhance detection rates.

Additionally, a small number of non-honeypot hosts were
identified. To enhance security, we recommend enforcing
key-based authentication (disabling passwords), changing the
default SSH port, restricting access via firewall rules, and
deploying tools like Fail2ban to block brute-force attacks.

3) SSL/TLS: Our analysis identified 20,391 SSL/TLS-
enabled addresses across 579 routing prefixes and 113 ASes.
A significant portion of FRPs utilize shared certificates, par-
ticularly from Cloudflare (e.g., Cloudflare issues certificates
for its domain cdnjs.cloudflare.com and massively deploys the

certificates under its FRP) and other providers employing wild-
card certificates (*.xxxx.com). While this approach streamlines
HTTPS content delivery and reduces certificate management
costs, it introduces potential risks - a single compromised
private key could affect multiple nodes. Certificate validity
analysis shows most vendors comply with industry standards
for short validity periods. However, for the few with extended
validity, we recommend implementing automated certificate
management systems for FRPs. Additional details are available
in Appendix Figure 9b.

VI. ETHICAL CONSIDERATIONS

In order to avoid impacting the real Internet [42], [43], all
our experiments are carried out at a single measurement point
using a single host, and the scanning speed is controlled at
1 Mbps. With different BGP routing prefixes, our scanning
requests are dispersed at different times during the test time
period, and the destination addresses are completely broken
up, and at the same time, a single scanning request is not more
than 10 minutes. The experiments provide an exit mechanism,
and for ASes that do not wish to be detected by the scan,
we include them in the blocking table and do not conduct the
experiments. In order to avoid attacks on real organizations and
network infrastructures, for possible vulnerabilities, we only
judged by fingerprint discrimination and version information,
and did not perform any penetration tests on real hosts, etc.,
to verify the existence of vulnerabilities.

VII. CONCLUSION

In this paper, we propose an autoregressive model-based
multi-protocol unified FRP probing algorithm named Gungnir,
which aims to address the network asset scanning challenges
posed by FRPs in IPv6 networks. Gungnir uses autoregres-
sive modeling to learn FRP pattern representations through
semantics by capturing the relationships among FRPs. With
the same budget, Gungnir improves the probing efficiency
by nearly 27 times, extends the coverage space by 4.2 times,
and covers more autonomous systems and routing prefixes to
achieve SOTA performance. Additional experiments validate
the effectiveness of the hyperparameter and granularity cor-
rection mechanisms. To facilitate better community research
in active addresses and FRPs, this paper makes the code and
all data publicly available and constructs a list of the most
extensive FRPs containing multiple active types. Furthermore,
it provides new ideas for Internet asset scanning and network
security analysis.
